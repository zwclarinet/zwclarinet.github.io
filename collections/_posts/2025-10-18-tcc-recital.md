On **October 14, 2025**, I had the privilege of presenting a 45-minute **guest artist lecture-recital** at **Tarrant County College Northwest Campus**. It was a wonderful evening filled with music, conversation, and connection—with both faculty and students.

The recital was structured around **five single-movement works**, each from a different musical period, and each introduced briefly to help guide listeners through the historical and stylistic context. The program aimed to highlight the **virtuosity and expressive range** of the clarinet.

---

### 🎼 Program Overview & Commentary

#### Carl Maria von Weber  
**Grand Duo Concertant, Op. 48 – I. Allegro con fuoco**

Weber helped carry the clarinet forward from the Classical into the Romantic era. While Mozart established the clarinet’s place in the orchestra, Weber gave it operatic power. In this piece, the clarinet sings like a soloist on stage, with the piano as an equal partner. The movement is full of bold contrasts, lyricism, and drama—a true duo in every sense.

---

#### Johannes Brahms  
**Clarinet Sonata No. 2 in E-flat Major, Op. 120 – II. Allegro appassionato**

Brahms had nearly retired from composing in his late 50s, but the playing of clarinetist Richard Mühlfeld inspired him to write four final masterpieces for the instrument. This sonata is one of them. The movement I chose is compact yet filled with emotional depth—passionate, intense, and reflective of Brahms’s mature style. It’s a beautiful example of "absolute music": pure expression, no narrative needed.

---

#### Francis Poulenc  
**Sonata for Clarinet and Piano, FP 184 – III. Allegro con fuoco**

In the 20th century, many composers experimented with new forms—Debussy went dreamy, Schoenberg went atonal—but Poulenc looked back. Associated with neoclassicism, Poulenc balanced formal clarity with bright, modern harmonies. This sonata was one of his last works, written for the legendary Benny Goodman. Although Poulenc passed away before its premiere, Goodman performed it with Leonard Bernstein at Carnegie Hall. The third movement bursts with color, rhythm, and wit.

---

#### Jörg Widmann  
**Fantasie for Clarinet Solo**

Composed in 1993, Widmann’s *Fantasie* has quickly become a modern classic. It’s often featured in competitions—not just because it’s difficult, but because it showcases the clarinet’s extended capabilities. From **multiphonics** (playing more than one note at once), to **flutter-tonguing**, **glissandos**, and other unusual effects, this piece stretches the instrument to its expressive limits. It’s wild, imaginative, and thrilling to perform.

---

#### Adolf Schreiner  
**Immer Kleiner (Always Smaller)**

We ended the program with a bit of humor. *Immer Kleiner* doesn’t come with deep historical significance—it's simply fun. The clarinet literally gets smaller during the piece (you’ll know what I mean if you’ve seen it!). It’s a light-hearted way to wrap up the recital and remind us that music can be joyful, theatrical, and surprising.

### 🎹 Collaboration and Planning

I was joined by the amazing pianist **Daria Kiselesa**, a DMA student at **Texas Christian University**, whose sensitivity and musicality made this recital truly special.

The event was made possible thanks to **Director Benson Lee**, who began coordinating with me back in **late August**. I’m so thankful for his trust and the warm welcome from the TCC faculty and staff.

---

### 💬 Q&A and Student Engagement

After the performance, we had an open **Q&A session**. The students asked truly thoughtful questions—about clarinet techniques, musical interpretation, and even career advice. It was a great reminder that performance is not just about playing, but about **sharing** and **connecting**.

As a performer and educator, I was genuinely happy to have the chance to explain the stories behind the music and engage with such an attentive and curious audience.

---

### ✨ Final Reflections

This guest artist recital wasn’t just a performance—it was a chance to create dialogue, spark curiosity, and celebrate the clarinet’s expressive power.

> It reminded me that sometimes, the most meaningful performances are the ones that invite others to **listen deeply**, **ask questions**, and **discover something new**.

Thank you again to everyone at **TCC Northwest** who made this possible. I had a fantastic evening—and I hope the students did too!
